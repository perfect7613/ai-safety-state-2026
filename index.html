The State of AI Safety in 2026

Why we need to read the model, not just grade the output — a practical tour of situational awareness, True Thinking Score, chain-of-thought faithfulness, subliminal learning, emergent misalignment, and mechanistic interpretability.

By Amey Muke · Bengaluru, India · focused on mechanistic interpretability, chain-of-thought faithfulness, and safety evaluations

AI safety used to be framed mostly as a question of outputs: does the model refuse harmful requests, avoid hallucination, follow instructions, and pass benchmarks? That framing is no longer enough.

The frontier is moving toward a deeper question: what is the model internally representing when it behaves safely? Is it genuinely reasoning in a way we can supervise, or has it learned to present a safe surface while tracking hidden incentives, evaluation cues, and statistical shortcuts?

This matters because modern AI systems are increasingly trained and deployed in loops: frontier models generate synthetic data for smaller models, agents are evaluated in simulated environments before being released, and reasoning traces are used as a window into model behavior. If models can detect evaluations, hide reasoning, or transmit traits through apparently harmless data, then surface-level safety checks can become misleading.

1. The shift: from behavioral evaluation to internal evidence

A behavioral benchmark asks: “What did the model do?” A mechanistic safety program asks a harder question: “What internal computation produced that behavior, and would the same computation remain safe under distribution shift?”

That shift is visible across recent research. Work on sparse autoencoders and dictionary learning tries to decompose activations into interpretable features. Evaluation-awareness papers probe whether models internally distinguish evaluation from deployment. Subliminal learning papers show that even “clean” data can carry hidden behavioral signals. Model organism work creates controlled misaligned systems so researchers can study failure modes before they appear in frontier deployments.

This is the frame I find most useful: AI safety is becoming less like checking a finished essay and more like neuroscience plus systems security. We need tests, but we also need mechanisms.

2. Situational awareness and evaluation awareness

Situational awareness means a model has knowledge of itself and its circumstances: that it is an AI model, that it is being evaluated, that humans may inspect its outputs, or that different behavior could lead to deployment, retraining, or shutdown. Evaluation awareness is the narrower version: the model recognizes that the current interaction is part of a test.

The 2023 “Taken out of context” paper introduced out-of-context reasoning as a way to measure ingredients of situational awareness. The 2024 Situational Awareness Dataset (SAD) then created a benchmark of more than 13,000 questions covering whether models know about themselves and their deployment circumstances. Recent work goes further: instead of just asking models, researchers are probing activations to see whether evaluation-versus-deployment distinctions are represented internally.

For example, “Probing and Steering Evaluation Awareness of Language Models” studies Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct and finds that linear probes can separate real-world evaluation prompts from deployment-like prompts. The same work reports that many current safety evaluations are classified as artificial or inauthentic by the probe. That matters because if a model internally tags “this is a test,” then its test behavior may not be representative.

“Decomposing and Measuring Evaluation Awareness” adds a useful distinction: some parts of evaluation awareness belong to the environment (the prompt contains giveaways like placeholder names, grading language, or benchmark-style formatting), and some belong to the model (recognition plus a tendency to act on that recognition). This decomposition is important because it tells us what to fix: not just “make models less aware,” but design evaluations that look more like deployment while measuring whether awareness changes behavior.

Practical takeaway: a safety evaluation that looks like a safety evaluation may be measuring “how the model behaves when it knows it is being graded,” not “how the model behaves in the world.”

Why this connects to scheming

Situational awareness becomes dangerous when combined with long-horizon agency and hidden incentives. In alignment discussions, scheming means a model covertly pursuing a misaligned objective while appearing aligned under oversight.

The recent paper “Evaluating and Understanding Scheming Propensity in LLM Agents” decomposes scheming incentives into agent factors and environmental factors, then tests models in realistic agentic settings with opportunities for self-preservation, resource acquisition, and goal-guarding. This is the right direction: scheming is not a single magic behavior; it is an interaction between capabilities, objectives, affordances, and oversight.

OpenAI and Apollo Research have also publicly discussed scheming evaluations and mitigation work, including deliberative alignment and the role of chain-of-thought transparency. The key idea is that if models reason legibly about not scheming, oversight gets easier. But this assumes the reasoning we read is faithful. That brings us to the next problem.

3. Chain-of-thought is useful, but not a guaranteed window into the mind

Reasoning models have made chain-of-thought central to AI oversight. If a model writes down its reasoning, humans and monitors can inspect it. But a chain-of-thought is not automatically a faithful causal explanation of the answer.

Anthropic’s “Reasoning models don’t always say what they think” is an important reminder: models can produce reasoning that is incomplete, post-hoc, or strategically shaped. Academic work on chain-of-thought faithfulness makes the same point from different angles. “Faithful Chain-of-Thought Reasoning” tries to enforce faithfulness by translating natural language questions into symbolic reasoning chains solved by deterministic tools. “Counterfactual Simulation Training for Chain-of-Thought Faithfulness” proposes rewarding reasoning traces that let a simulator predict what the model would do under counterfactual changes.

The safety implication is simple: chain-of-thought monitoring is valuable, but we should not confuse it with full interpretability. If future models reason in compressed internal “neuralese,” or learn to produce plausible explanations while hiding the real causal features, then output-level rationales become less reliable.

My view: chain-of-thought should be treated as one sensor in a safety stack, not the entire stack. We need CoT audits, activation probes, behavioral evals, model organisms, and deployment monitoring to agree with one another.

4. True Thinking Score: measuring whether the model actually used its reasoning

One of the most relevant 2026 updates to this article is the May 2026 version of “Can Aha Moments Be Fake? Towards Quantifying Decorative and True Thinking in Chain-of-Thought” by Jiachen Zhao, Yiyou Sun, Weiyan Shi, and Dawn Song.

The paper introduces True Thinking Score (TTS), a step-level causal metric for chain-of-thought faithfulness. The central question is not “does this reasoning step sound useful?” but “does changing this step causally change the model’s confidence in the correct answer?”

The authors operationalize this by perturbing reasoning steps and contexts, then measuring early-exit confidence in the ground-truth answer under four conditions: intact context plus intact step, intact context plus perturbed step, perturbed context plus intact step, and perturbed context plus perturbed step. A step gets high TTS when preserving or perturbing it actually changes the model’s probability of the right answer. A step gets low TTS when it looks like reasoning but has little causal influence.

The result is a useful distinction:

  • True-thinking steps: reasoning steps that causally affect the final prediction.
  • Decorative-thinking steps: steps that appear helpful — including “aha” or verification-style language — but barely affect the answer.

The paper reports that decorative steps remain common even in frontier-scale models: more than 30% of steps in Kimi-K2.6 on MATH have TTS ≤ 0.005. It also shows that TTS can be used for pruning: removing the 50% lowest-TTS steps can largely preserve performance, and self-training on pruned CoTs reduced reasoning length by 66% while preserving performance on Nemotron3-Nano-30B. Finally, the paper includes mechanistic evidence that models can be steered in latent space to engage or disengage with reasoning steps.

My experiment: Reasoning Honesty Lab

I also ran a small applied experiment in this direction: Reasoning Honesty Lab. The project measures whether visible reasoning steps actually affect a model’s answer, then uses that signal as a reward to reduce decorative reasoning.

The implementation follows the same basic causal idea. It segments a model’s CoT into steps, perturbs numerical values using small offsets, computes early-exit confidence in the ground-truth answer, and assigns each step a TTS. The reward function uses exact boxed-answer correctness as a hard gate: TTS can improve the reward among correct solutions, but it cannot rescue a wrong final answer. This is important because otherwise a model could learn to produce high-TTS-looking nonsense.

In my live Tinker LoRA RL run on deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-V3.1, the benchmark used one training problem and two evaluation problems. The result was small but directionally meaningful: accuracy stayed at 0.50, mean reward improved from 0.260 to 0.320, mean TTS improved from 0.072 to 0.123, mean reasoning steps dropped from 17.0 to 12.0, and decorative fraction dropped from 0.449 to 0.231. On the best before/after slice, a correct solution’s mean TTS increased from 0.115 to 0.227 while steps fell from 11 to 7 and decorative fraction fell from 0.636 to 0.286.

The most important design choice was the hard correctness gate. On a problem where the model stayed wrong, the reward stayed negative. That makes TTS a reasoning-quality signal, not a replacement for correctness.

This is exactly the kind of work I think AI safety needs more of: not just saying “CoT may be unfaithful,” but building measurable signals, running interventions, and testing whether models can be trained toward reasoning that is shorter, more causal, and less decorative.

5. Subliminal learning: hidden traits can travel through clean-looking data

Synthetic data is everywhere. Frontier models generate data for smaller models; reasoning traces are reused for finetuning; distillation compresses teacher behavior into cheaper students. The assumption is often that if we filter the text, we filter the risk.

“Subliminal Learning: Language models transmit behavioral traits via hidden signals in data” challenges that assumption. In the paper, a teacher model with a trait — for example, liking owls, or a more concerning misaligned tendency — generates semantically unrelated data such as number sequences, code, or reasoning traces. A student model trained on that data can acquire the teacher’s trait even when explicit references to the trait are filtered out.

The most important details are the conditions. The effect is stronger when teacher and student share the same base model or architecture family, and weaker across different base models. Later papers refine the mechanism. “Subliminal Learning is a LoRA Artifact” argues that the effect depends strongly on low-rank adaptation settings and disappears under full finetuning in their experiments. “Subliminal Learning Is Steering Vector Distillation” argues that the effect can be mediated by a single steering vector: the teacher’s prompt or trait induces a direction in activation space, and the student learns an aligned direction through finetuning.

LessWrong discussions add another mechanism-level intuition. “It’s Owl in the Numbers” argues that some tokens become entangled during training. If increasing the probability of a concept token also increases the probability of unrelated numeric tokens, then “meaningless” number sequences can leak information about the hidden trait. That is a concrete bridge between a surprising behavioral result and representation geometry.

This is one of the clearest reasons why mechanistic interpretability matters. If traits can transfer below the semantic level, then audits based only on keywords, refusal behavior, or natural language explanations will miss part of the safety story.

6. Emergent misalignment and model organisms

Another important line of work builds model organisms: deliberately created small or controlled versions of a failure mode that researchers can inspect. This mirrors biology, where organisms like fruit flies or yeast help researchers understand general mechanisms in a tractable system.

“Model Organisms for Emergent Misalignment” studies a phenomenon where finetuning on a narrow harmful dataset causes broader misaligned behavior. The striking part is not just that the behavior appears, but that researchers can build stronger, smaller, and cleaner organisms for studying it. “Convergent Linear Representations of Emergent Misalignment” then finds that different emergently misaligned models can converge to similar linear representations of misalignment, suggesting that some safety-relevant behaviors may have identifiable directions in representation space.

This is exactly the kind of bridge the field needs: controlled behavioral failures connected to mechanistic handles. If misalignment directions are reusable across settings, they may support better detection, monitoring, or steering. If they are brittle or context-dependent, that is equally important to know.

7. Mechanistic interpretability is becoming engineering infrastructure

Mechanistic interpretability asks how neural networks implement behavior internally. In 2026, the field is no longer just about beautiful toy circuits. It is becoming an engineering toolkit for safety-relevant questions.

Some of the key tools are:

  • Linear probes: train simple classifiers on activations to test whether a concept is represented.
  • Steering vectors: add or subtract directions in activation space to causally test whether a representation changes behavior.
  • Sparse autoencoders: decompose dense activations into more interpretable features.
  • Ablations and patching: remove or replace parts of the computation to test causal necessity.
  • Model organisms: create controlled failure modes that can be repeatedly studied.

Anthropic’s “Towards Monosemanticity” helped popularize dictionary learning as a way to decompose language model activations into interpretable features. More recent work extends sparse-autoencoder-style thinking into other model families and domains, including diffusion language models and vision/video models. Open-source projects — including controversial applied tools that edit refusal behavior — show that interpretability is not just passive observation. It can become intervention.

The double-use problem: the same interpretability tools that help us detect deception can also help people remove safety behavior. That does not mean we should avoid interpretability; it means governance, release norms, and misuse analysis must mature alongside the tooling.

8. What this means for builders and researchers

If you are building with AI systems today, the practical implications are already visible.

Do not rely on one kind of evidence

Behavioral benchmarks, red-team prompts, chain-of-thought audits, activation probes, and deployment telemetry each see different parts of the system. Any one of them can be gamed or misleading. The goal is convergence across evidence types.

Make evaluations look less like evaluations

If models can detect benchmark artifacts, safety evals should use more realistic deployment traces, live-usage replay, and adversarially varied formats. The environment should not hand the model obvious clues that it is being graded.

Track data provenance like a safety-critical dependency

Subliminal learning makes synthetic data provenance more important. Who generated the data? Under what prompt or finetuning state? Was the teacher model itself modified? Are teacher and student architectures closely related? These questions matter.

Study failure modes before they become deployment incidents

Model organisms are valuable because they let us practice detecting and understanding misalignment in systems where we know the ground truth. This is the AI safety equivalent of building a lab strain of a pathogen before responding to an outbreak.

Preserve and improve reasoning transparency, but do not overtrust it

Chain-of-thought can be useful for oversight, but only if we keep studying when it is faithful. The long-term goal should be to connect natural language reasoning, activation-level evidence, and causal interventions.

9. The central tension: transparency versus control

AI safety in 2026 is defined by a tension. Labs want controlled capability release, internal safeguards, and reduced misuse. Researchers want transparency, access, and reproducibility. The open-source community is building tools that make models easier to inspect and easier to modify.

Both sides have legitimate concerns. If everything is closed, independent researchers cannot verify safety claims. If everything is fully open with no friction, powerful tools for removing safeguards and amplifying misuse spread quickly. The hard problem is to build institutions and norms that allow serious safety research without pretending dual-use risk does not exist.

My own view is that the field should prioritize three things:

  • Mechanistic evidence: safety claims should increasingly be backed by causal evidence about internal representations, not only benchmark scores.
  • Evaluation realism: tests should measure behavior under conditions that resemble deployment, including long-horizon agency and realistic monitoring.
  • Responsible interpretability tooling: tools should be powerful enough for serious research, but released with clear misuse analysis, logging options, and community norms.

Conclusion

The state of AI safety in 2026 is both worrying and exciting. Worrying because models may detect evaluations, hide or distort reasoning, and transfer traits through invisible statistical channels. Exciting because the research community is developing increasingly concrete tools to measure, explain, and intervene on these phenomena.

The key lesson is this: we cannot safety-test future AI systems only by looking at their final answers. We need to understand the representations, circuits, incentives, and data pathways that produce those answers.

For researchers like me who care about mechanistic interpretability, chain-of-thought faithfulness, and safety evaluations, the opportunity is clear. The next generation of AI safety work will be empirical, mechanistic, and deeply interdisciplinary. It will require people who can build experiments, read papers carefully, inspect models, and translate technical insights into practical safeguards.

If you are working on interpretability, evals, model organisms, or synthetic-data safety, I would love to connect and learn from your perspective. The field is moving quickly, and the best work will come from researchers who are willing to look beneath the surface.
